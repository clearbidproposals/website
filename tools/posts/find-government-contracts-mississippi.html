Government work in Mississippi comes from three places: federal agencies, state agencies, and local governments like counties, cities and school districts. Each one posts bids in a different place and has its own registration. Here is where to look, what to sign up for, and the rule that decides whether a job is advertised at all.

Start with the basics every buyer expects

Before you chase a specific bid, get three things in place. They take a few weeks, and you will need them for almost every opportunity below.

  • Know your NAICS codes. NAICS codes classify a business by the products or services it supplies. Federal buyers use them to find contractors and to decide who counts as a small business. Look yours up on the Census Bureau's NAICS site, then check the SBA size standard for each code.
  • Register in SAM.gov. SAM.gov is the system federal agencies use to find and pay contractors, and registering gives you a Unique Entity ID (UEI). It is free. SAM.gov says there is no charge to get a UEI, register, or keep your registration current, so be wary of anyone selling registration. Allow at least ten business days after you submit for it to become active, and renew it every 365 days. You will need your Taxpayer Identification Number, bank details for electronic payment, and a physical address (P.O. boxes are not accepted).
  • Have a capability statement ready. This is a one-page summary of what you do, your past work, your codes and certifications, and how to reach you. Buyers and prime contractors ask for it constantly. Here is a sample capability statement we wrote for a fictional company.

Federal contracts performed in Mississippi

Federal agencies post their open solicitations on SAM.gov under Contract Opportunities. Search by your NAICS codes and filter for Mississippi as the place of performance. If you sign in, you can save the search and get an email when new matches are posted.

If your business qualifies for an SBA program (8(a), HUBZone, women-owned or veteran-owned), it is worth getting certified. SBA notes that agencies reserve contracts for small businesses certified in its contracting programs, so on a contract set aside for your program, you compete only against other certified firms.

State of Mississippi agencies

State agencies buy through MAGIC, Mississippi's eProcurement system, which is run by the Department of Finance and Administration (DFA). Register as a supplier through DFA's vendor registration page. Once you are registered, you can receive notices of new solicitations (the state calls them RFxs), search upcoming ones, respond electronically and receive purchase orders.

Open solicitations are also listed on the state's Buying and Selling to Government in Mississippi page, which links to procurement opportunities and public notices, and on DFA's solicitations page.

Technology purchases go through ITS

Information technology is bought differently. Mississippi's Department of Information Technology Services (ITS) lists its RFPs on its RFPs and Sole Sources page and also advertises them in The Clarion-Ledger, usually on Tuesdays. ITS says responses need an MDA Minority & Small Business form and an IRS Form W-9.

The dollar thresholds that decide what gets advertised

Mississippi's purchasing law, Section 31-7-13 of the Mississippi Code, applies to state agencies and to local governing authorities such as counties, cities and school districts. It sets three tiers:

Purchase amountWhat the law requires
$5,000 or lessNo advertising or competitive bids required
Over $5,000, up to $75,000At least two competitive written bids (quotes), with no advertisement
Over $75,000Advertised for competitive bids once a week for two consecutive weeks

The middle tier matters more than most contractors realize. Jobs between $5,000 and $75,000 don't have to be advertised, so the buyer often just asks for quotes from vendors it already knows. To get those calls, introduce yourself to the purchasing offices of the agencies, counties and cities you want to work for, send your capability statement, and ask to be added to their vendor list for your trade.

Counties, cities and school districts

Local governments advertise their larger bids in the legal notices of a local newspaper, and many post them on their own websites as well. Some also accept bids electronically. For example, a June 2025 Lauderdale County bid notice, published in The Meridian Star, said bidders could download the specifications from the county's website or from Central Bidding, submit electronically through Central Bidding, or deliver a sealed bid by mail, courier or hand.

A simple weekly routine works well. List the counties, cities, school districts and universities in the area you can serve. Bookmark each one's bid or purchasing page, register on any electronic bidding service they use, and scan your local paper's legal notices once a week.

Free help is available

APEX Accelerators, formerly called Procurement Technical Assistance Centers (PTACs), help businesses sell to federal, state and local governments. According to SBA, they can help you decide whether you are ready for government contracting, register in the required systems, check whether you qualify for small business certifications, and research past contract opportunities. Mississippi's program took the name Mississippi APEX Accelerator in 2023. Find the office nearest you with the APEX Accelerator locator.

Before you submit any bid

  • Read everything. Read the entire solicitation and every amendment (also called an addendum). Changes to dates, quantities and required forms often appear only in amendments.
  • Track every requirement. List each requirement and form the solicitation asks for, and check them off before you submit. This list is called a compliance matrix, and it is the simplest way to avoid being thrown out on a technicality.
  • Follow the submission instructions exactly. That means format, number of copies, where and how to deliver, and the deadline. Late bids are generally not accepted.
