A capability statement is a one-page summary of what your company does, the work you have done, and the codes and certifications a buyer needs to see. Government buyers and prime contractors often ask for one. Here is what goes in each section, shown with our sample capability statement. We wrote the sample for Cedar Hollow Facility Services, a fictional janitorial company. Its people, clients and ID numbers are made up too.

What a capability statement is for

A guide posted on HUD's website calls it a marketing document about your capabilities, tailored to a government buyer or a prime contractor. The agency guides agree on a few basics:

  • Keep it to one page. An NIH guide says one page, one side if possible. HHS says one page, front and back. The Department of the Interior says it can run onto a second page but should not go over two.
  • Make it easy to scan. HHS recommends bullets and simple tables. The HUD guide favors short sentences and bullet points over long paragraphs.
  • Call it a Capability Statement. The HUD and NIH guides both say to title it that way, as our sample does.

The sections, one at a time

The HUD guide lists five content areas. Our sample covers all five, plus a short overview at the top. They match the sections on our capability statements page.

1. Company overview

Open with two or three short sentences that connect what you do to the buyer's needs, as NIH recommends. Our sample opens: "Cedar Hollow provides nightly janitorial, floor care and disinfection services for public buildings, medical offices and multi-tenant commercial facilities." In one sentence the reader knows the trade, the kind of buildings and the kind of client.

2. Core competencies

This is a bulleted list of the work you do. The HUD guide recommends keyword-rich bullets that cover your core expertise for that buyer, not everything you can do. Our sample lists six competencies, and each names a specific service, such as "Floor care: stripping and refinishing, weekly buffing, carpet extraction" or "Two-hour response for urgent requests during contract hours." A contractor would name its trades, systems and project types the same way, rather than a broad label.

3. Differentiators

Differentiators tell the buyer why to pick you over the next bidder. The HUD guide warns that if buyers cannot see your advantages quickly, they have no basis for choosing you. NIH adds that each differentiator should tie to a need of the agency, prime contractor or teaming partner you are approaching.

Make them concrete enough to check. Our sample's first differentiator reads: "Documented quality. Weekly inspections on a 25-point checklist, with a monthly report of scores, issues and corrective actions." A buyer can ask to see that report. A phrase like "committed to quality" gives them nothing to check.

4. Past performance

Interior's guidance notes that contracting officers need to verify you have completed similar work. The HUD and NIH guides both suggest listing government work before commercial work, and leaving out jobs that don't relate to the buyer.

Our sample uses a small table with four columns: client, scope, period and result. One row reads: Ashford County Courthouse, 32,000 sq ft, nightly service, 2022 to present, renewed twice with zero missed shifts in 2025. The client is fictional, but the format works for any trade. Report results you can back up, such as renewals, completion dates or inspection scores.

  • Construction can reach back further. Interior's worksheet says federal and non-federal contracts both count. It asks for references generally from the past three years, but says six years may be considered for construction and architect-engineer contracts.
  • Add references where you can. NIH suggests a name, title, email and phone number for each.
  • New company? Interior suggests that startups emphasize the founder's expertise.

5. Company data

This block holds the facts a buyer uses to check you out. The HUD guide suggests a brief description with your firm's size, number of employees and typical service area, followed by your codes. Our sample fits it all in the right-hand column.

  • Drop the DUNS number. The HUD, NIH and Interior guides still ask for one, but GSA says SAM.gov no longer uses it to identify entities. Use your UEI.
  • Check every NAICS code. The HUD guide says to list the numbers only. Interior asks for each code's standard description. Either way, confirm each code on the Census Bureau's NAICS site.
  • List only certifications you hold. SBA says firms must apply for certification before they can compete for WOSB set-aside contracts. HHS suggests listing state and local certifications too. In Mississippi, MDOT takes part in the Mississippi Unified Certification Program for Disadvantaged Business Enterprise (DBE) certification and keeps a DBE directory.
  • Mention bonding if you have it. SBA notes that many public and private contracts require surety bonds.
  • Say if you accept purchase cards. The NIH guide lists acceptance of credit and purchase cards as company data to include.

6. Contact

Name a real person. The HUD guide recommends a specific person's name, a direct email address rather than a generic info@ address, a phone number and your website. Our sample lists the owner by name, with a direct phone number, email and street address. The HUD guide also says to keep your website current and focused on government buyers, because readers will visit it.

Format and file tips

  • Use the whole page. NIH suggests small margins, with color and graphics only where they help.
  • Send a PDF. NIH and HUD both say to save and send it as a PDF, not a Word or PowerPoint file. NIH suggests keeping the file under 1MB.
  • Name the file well. The HUD guide says to put your company's name in the file name.
  • Get a second reader. HHS suggests having someone outside your company review it, and checking spelling and grammar before you share it.

Tailor it, use it and keep it current

The HUD guide says a generic version is less effective. NIH suggests a new version for each agency, prime contractor or teaming opportunity, and Interior calls the capability statement a living document. In practice, keep one master file. For each buyer, adjust the overview, reorder the competencies and pick the past performance that matches their work.

Reuse the same words in SBA's Small Business Search (SBS), which replaced the Dynamic Small Business Search. The Air Force small business office calls SBS the official source federal agencies use to find SBA-certified and self-certified small businesses. SAM.gov passes only a limited profile to SBS, so APEX Accelerator trainers advise adding the capability narrative and keywords yourself. They call the narrative the most important part of the profile.

Then send it out. Our guide to finding government contracts in Mississippi explains why it pays to introduce yourself to purchasing offices and ask to be added to their vendor lists. For hands-on help, APEX Accelerators (formerly Procurement Technical Assistance Centers) provide technical assistance to businesses selling to federal, state and local buyers, and some have run capability statement workshops listed on SBA's events calendar.
