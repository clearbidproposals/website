A bid can lose before anyone compares prices. It arrives late, the bond is missing, or an amendment was never acknowledged. Here are the most common reasons, where each rule comes from, and how to avoid them. Federal sealed bids, federal proposals and Mississippi public bids follow different rules, so we keep them separate.

Three sets of rules

  • Federal sealed bidding (FAR Part 14). A bid that does not conform to the essential requirements of the invitation for bids must be rejected (FAR 14.404-2). Only minor informalities can be waived, and FAR 14.405 defines them narrowly.
  • Federal negotiated proposals (FAR Part 15). A proposal that misses a clearly stated, material requirement can be rated technically unacceptable. The standard instructions in FAR 52.215-1 say the government intends to award without discussions, so your first proposal "should contain the offeror's best terms."
  • Mississippi public bidding. State building projects run by the Bureau of Building, Grounds and Real Property Management follow the Bureau's rules, which list "Failure to comply with the bid requirements" as grounds to disqualify a low bidder. On a county, city or school district job, the advertisement and instructions to bidders are your rulebook.

The FAR is being rewritten under the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul, so always follow the provisions in your solicitation. This is general information, not legal advice. For legal questions, ask a lawyer.

1. Late submission

In federal buying, if the solicitation sets no time, the deadline is 4:30 p.m. local time (FAR 14.304 for bids, FAR 15.208 for proposals). A late submission counts only in narrow cases, such as an authorized electronic submission that reached the government's system by 5:00 p.m. one working day before the deadline, and only if it arrives before award.

GAO, which decides federal bid protests, puts it plainly: "It is an offeror's responsibility to deliver its proposal to the proper place at the proper time." In a 2025 case, product samples shipped four days before the deadline were delayed by weather, turned away by base security, and arrived one day late. GAO upheld the rejection.

Mississippi's Bureau of Building says late delivery "for any reason," including by mail or courier, disqualifies the bid.

How to avoid it: plan to submit one working day early. Test portal logins well ahead. For hand delivery, allow time for security and ask for a receipt that shows the time.

2. Unacknowledged amendments and addenda

Federal sealed bids

FAR 52.214-3 requires you to acknowledge every amendment by the time and place set for receipt of bids. You can sign and return it, list its number and date on the bid form, or send a letter. Under FAR 14.405, a missed acknowledgment can be waived only if the bid clearly shows you received the amendment, or the amendment has a negligible effect on price, quantity, quality or delivery.

In February 2026, GAO sustained a protest of an Army Corps of Engineers award because the low bidder had not acknowledged an amendment that called for a larger sheet pile cap. The agency put the cost at roughly 1 to 2 percent of the bid. GAO still found the amendment material because it changed what the contractor had to build, so the bid should have been rejected as nonresponsive.

Federal proposals

FAR 52.215-1 says to acknowledge amendments by the date and time each amendment specifies.

Mississippi Bureau of Building

The Bureau's rule says to acknowledge each addendum on the Proposal Form, and addenda become part of the contract.

How to avoid it: check the posting site the day before and the morning of the deadline. List every amendment number and date, and make sure your price reflects each change.

3. A missing or defective bid bond

Federal

A contracting officer may require a bid guarantee only when a performance bond, or performance and payment bonds, are also required (FAR 28.101-1). For construction, only a separate bid guarantee is acceptable. The amount must be at least 20 percent of the bid price, up to $3 million (FAR 28.101-2).

In sealed bidding, a bid without the required guarantee must be rejected unless a narrow exception in FAR 28.101-4 applies, such as an otherwise acceptable bond that is misdated. In a negotiated buy awarded without discussions, a noncompliant proposal is rejected as unacceptable.

Mississippi Bureau of Building

The sealed envelope must hold the Bid Proposal Form and the bid security. Bid security must be at least 5 percent: a certified check payable to the Bureau, or a bid bond for 5 percent of the base bid from a surety licensed in Mississippi.

How to avoid it: call your surety as soon as you decide to bid. If the bond states a dollar amount, check it against your final price. Make sure the bidder name and project on the bond match the bid.

4. Missing signatures and added conditions

In federal sealed bidding, an unsigned bid can be excused only if other material shows you meant to be bound, such as a bid guarantee, or your firm authorized a typed or stamped signature before bid opening (FAR 14.405). For proposals, FAR 52.215-1 says the first page must include the signer's name, title and signature.

Do not add your own terms. FAR 14.404-2 requires rejecting a bid that adds conditions that change the requirements or limit the bidder's liability, such as pricing tied to the price in effect at delivery. If something looks wrong, ask the contracting officer before the deadline.

In Mississippi, the Bureau calls itself "the sole judge in determining irregularities, or informalities." Do not count on a missing form being excused.

5. Not registered or not licensed

Federal: SAM.gov

FAR 52.204-7 requires you to be registered in SAM when you submit an offer and at award, unless the solicitation uses an alternate version that allows later registration. Your Unique Entity ID goes on the offer's cover page. SAM.gov says you must renew every 365 days, so confirm you are active before each bid. Our guide to finding government contracts in Mississippi covers registration.

Mississippi: certificate of responsibility

The Bureau's rule says you need a certificate of responsibility number to submit a bid of more than $50,000, shown on the bid envelope or with an electronic submission. For a bid of $50,000 or less, put a statement saying so on the face of the envelope. The State Board of Contractors' rule tells agencies to return bids unopened from contractors who lack the required certificate.

How to avoid it: before you price a job, confirm that your certificate is current and covers the classification of work.

6. Page limits and format rules

Page limits matter most in negotiated proposals, and GAO has upheld agencies that enforce them.

  • In January 2026, an Air Force construction RFP set a 100-page limit and said excess pages "will not be read or considered." One offeror sent 112 pages. The agency dropped the last 12, which held required information, and rated the proposal technically unacceptable. GAO denied the protest.
  • In a 2020 Army Corps of Engineers procurement, a 36-page volume ran one page over a limit that counted the cover page. The dropped page held a required organizational chart. GAO said offerors that exceed page limits "assume the risk that the agency will not consider the excess pages."

How to avoid it: read exactly what counts toward each limit, and check font, margin and file rules. A compliance matrix lists every instruction so you can check each one off.

A short pre-submission checklist

Run this list on every bid, ideally with a second reader such as a colleague or a bid review service.

  • Deadline, delivery method and address confirmed, with a plan to submit a day early.
  • Every amendment or addendum acknowledged and priced.
  • Bid security in the required form and amount, with matching names.
  • Bid form complete and signed by someone authorized, with no added conditions.
  • Federal: SAM.gov active and UEI on the cover page. Mississippi: certificate of responsibility number, or the $50,000-or-less statement, on the envelope.
  • Page counts and format checked, and every required form included.
